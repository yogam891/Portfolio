Portfolio Statement

Everything here comes from one writing course, ENGW 104, but it's really the story of one question changing. I started out wanting to write about death and whether we have a soul. The more I read, the more that turned into a different question: what makes you the same person you were yesterday, or a year ago? Every piece shows a step in that change, from the journal entries where I checked in on how the work was going, to the planning assignments, to the final essay.

I want you to think about your own answer while you read. I don't land on a clean one, cause I don't have one. What the pieces show is each theory I looked at breaking in the same kind of place, and that something is still there even when a character has too much memory or almost none.

Read together, I see someone who thinks by asking questions out loud and got more comfortable with not knowing. I also see my writing loosening up. Some of the earlier pieces are more formal, and the essay sounds more like me, with a poem at the end that I left for you to read your own way. I started this class with questions about death. I'm finishing it with the same questions, asked about myself.

Core Essay

So, Who Am I, Really?

An Essay On Personal Identity

About this piece

This is the main piece of the portfolio, my core essay. It asks what personal identity is and goes through Locke, Parfit, and Roache, the Ship of Theseus, and my own idea that identity works like an integral. It then tests all of that on a real chatbot built from a dead man's texts and on two characters from fiction, one with too much memory and one with almost none. It doesn't end with a clean answer. It ends with a poem I wrote, which I left unexplained on purpose.

The piece, as I wrote it

Am I the same person I was a year ago? Yesterday? An hour ago? I don’t know. I’m not even sure I’m the same person around my mom as I am around my friends, and that’s on the same afternoon. I’ve been reading people who are supposed to know this stuff, and they don’t agree with each other. A soul, memory, something else. Everybody’s got a different answer.

This all started with death, actually. I wanted to write about death and whether we have a soul or not. I’ve lost my father and my grandmother, and I almost died myself once (appendicitis, and I didn’t even understand what was happening), so death never felt like some far away topic to me. But the more I read about the soul, the more I got stuck on a different question. If a soul does carry on after you die, what exactly is it carrying? Is that still me? And what even is “me” to begin with? That second question started pulling harder than the first one, so that’s what this essay ended up being about.

I’m not gonna give one clean answer, cause I don’t have one. What I’m gonna do is go through what a few philosophers actually say, throw some hard cases at their ideas and see what holds up.

Locke’s Viewpoint

Locke is where I’ll start, cause he’s the one who really pulls these ideas apart. He says “man” and “person” are two different things. A man is just the animal. It’s the living body, and it counts as the same man from birth to death even though the stuff it’s made of keeps changing. A person is something else. Locke defines a person as “a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places”. There’s no body in that definition. No soul either. A person is basically the thing that can look back at the past and say, yeah, that was me.

He also calls “person” a forensic term. Basically it’s a courtroom word. It exists so we can decide who gets praised or blamed, rewarded or punished. And it only makes sense to punish you for something you actually know you did, something you can remember as yours. That’s why memory ends up at the center of his whole theory.

Then he gives the prince and the cobbler. Say the prince’s soul, with all of his memories, gets put into the cobbler’s body, and the cobbler’s own soul is gone. Locke says the one walking around in the cobbler’s body is the prince. Not somebody who thinks he’s the prince. He is the prince. Everybody else would just see a cobbler. The body is still the same man it always was, so “same man” and “same person” come apart. Memory decides which one you follow.

Then he flips it. What if a soul carries over but none of the memories do? Locke says that’s not the same person. Same soul, different person. So a soul by itself doesn’t get you anything. Even if souls exist, even if they last forever, that alone doesn’t tell you the person on the other side is you.

That part surprised me, cause I came into this thinking the soul would be the answer. Locke basically says it isn’t. But I’ve got a problem with his answer too. He says I’m the same person as my past self if I remember being that self. But remembering something already means it happened to me. So it goes in a circle. I’m the same person as whoever I remember being, and “remember being” just means “was.” Roache points out the same thing, that defining personal identity through memory risks circularity. So I don’t think memory can be the whole answer either. It’s also kinda weird that a word built for courtrooms is the one we end up using to ask whether we survive death.

There’s also a smaller, dumber version of this question that I was stuck on way before I found any of these guys. I change. Every day. I’m not the same around my mom as I am around my friends. So is my identity not really one thing, but a pile of slightly different me’s, one for every second I’ve been alive? That kinda scared me when I first thought it through.

I’m not the first person to notice this. Heraclitus said it like 2,500 years ago, that you can’t step into the same river twice, cause the water’s moved on and so have you. Then there’s the Ship of Theseus. Replace every plank of a ship, one at a time, and at the end, is it still the same ship?

The problem is that a ship has nothing going on inside it. There’s nothing about what it’s like to be a boat. So, all the ship really shows is that a structure can stay the same through change. It doesn’t tell me if there’s somebody home the whole time. And that’s the part I care about.

Parfit gave me a better way to think about it. He splits it into two things. Psychological connectedness is a direct link between two points in your life, like remembering something. Psychological continuity is an overlapping chain of those links. Connectedness fades the further apart two moments are. Continuity doesn’t have to.

Think of a rope. No single fiber runs the whole length, but the fibers overlap, so the rope holds. That’s how I see it. I can be continuous with the guy I was ten years ago and have almost no direct connection with him. I don’t remember most of what he did, or how it felt. That doesn’t mean he isn’t me. It just means a lot of time has passed.

This next part is my own idea, so take it for what it is. If time is made of infinitely many tiny instants, then there’s infinitely many versions of me, one for every instant. That sounds like a problem. But calculus kind of has an answer for it. An integral adds up infinitely many super thin slices, each one worth almost nothing on its own, and still gives you one real, finite number. I think a personality works like that. And maybe in the way of life, we even leave behind some of our personal identities, which also adds or subtracts to our personal identity. So, no single moment is all of me. The sum of all of them is. So personal identity equals one. I know that’s an analogy and not a proof. But it fits what the rope already showed me. Identity isn’t any one point. It’s the whole stretch.

There’s also a different kind of “many me’s” that has nothing to do with time. William James said a person has as many social selves as there are people who carry an image of him in their head. Erving Goffman took that and ran with it. He said people perform different versions of themselves depending on who’s watching, kind of like actors playing different roles on different stages.

That’s basically me with my mom versus me with my friends. So the “who am I” question doesn’t only stretch across time. It stretches across every room I walk into.

So far that’s all slow change, the kind that happens to everybody whether they notice or not. The harder version is when somebody gets copied on purpose. Yeah, it sounds like science fiction and it is but let’s just go with it for a minute.

Parfit’s most famous example is a teleporter. A scanner on Earth records every cell in your body and destroys the original. A machine on Mars builds an exact copy from the data. Did you travel? Or did you die and get replaced by somebody who just thinks he’s you? Then he makes it worse. Say the scanner doesn’t destroy you right away. For a few days there are two of you, and only one is about to die. Both have the same claim to being you. Parfit’s conclusion is that strict identity isn’t what matters in survival. What matters is what he calls Relation R, which is basically psychological continuity (Parfit). And Relation R can go one-to-many, so there’s no clean answer to which one is really you.

Okay, so Roache takes up a related question. Can one person even have a memory of something that happened to somebody else? She calls that a quasi-memory. Say you copy one memory from Paul to Jane. Roache’s example is a memory of overhearing a funny conversation in Italian. Paul speaks Italian. Jane doesn’t. Does Jane get Paul’s amusement? To find it funny, she’d need to know Italian too.

So Roache splits it into two versions. In the narrow version, Jane only gets the sense impressions, what Paul saw and heard, and none of his feelings. In the broad version, she gets the feelings too. But then you run into Wollheim’s point, that memories are tied into a whole web of other stuff, like beliefs, emotions, and skills, so you can’t just lift one out cleanly. Either way, Roache says, copying memories piece by piece gives you something weaker than the original.

Then she looks at a different kind of copying, wholesale. Instead of one memory, you transfer a person’s whole mind, memories, character, habits, all of it. That’s what happens in Parfit’s brain-splitting case, where each half of a brain goes into a different body. The two people who wake up didn’t exist before the operation, so they have no mental stuff of their own to clash with what they inherit. The problems from piecemeal copying don’t show up, and Roache says that rescues the idea of quasi-memory.

Here’s where I get stuck, though. A person with no mental stuff of their own isn’t really a person waiting to receive something. They’re nothing yet. So, the one case that makes this work might not even make sense outside of a thought experiment. It only works because nobody’s home to argue with it.

All of that sounded like thought experiments until I found out somebody actually tried a small version of it.

In November 2015, a man named Roman Mazurenko was hit by a car in Moscow and died . His close friend Eugenia Kuyda ran a company that built chatbots. She collected his old text messages, more than 8,000 lines from ten of his friends and family, added hundreds of her own, and used them to build a bot that talked the way he did. Then she made it public, so anyone could chat with it. People reacted really differently though. A lot of people found it comforting. His friend Sergey Fayfer said the phrases the bot used were “really his”. But four of his friends were disturbed by it and wouldn’t touch it. And Kuyda herself struggled with whether she was doing the right thing, and the project sometimes even gave her nightmares.

Black Mirror has an episode, “Be Right Back,” that tells almost the same story. A woman named Martha loses her boyfriend Ash in a car accident. She signs up for a service that rebuilds him from his online history. At First it’s only messages. Then it’s his voice on the phone. And then it’s a physical android. Eventually she tests it, and it just goes along with whatever she says. It doesn’t push back and argue. It doesn’t act scared, which Ash would have. Once she told him to go die and he went and nearly did that, Which a real human would never have done. That’s how she knows it isn’t him.

By Roache’s definitions, both of these are piecemeal cases. A bunch of texts, a bunch of recorded habits. That’s scraps from the outside, not the whole mind behind them. So even being as generous as possible, I don’t think either one is the same person, not by Locke’s standard and not by Parfit’s. Maybe that’s why the bot felt right to some people and wrong to others. It sounds like him. It just isn’t all of him. And that’s what makes this hit different from the thought experiments. Real people sat there messaging it, and they felt something real, whether that was comfort or the creeps.

Two more places where I ran into this question were in things I was reading anyway, a web novel and a Russian novella, and they sit at opposite ends.

In the web novel A Regressor’s Tale of Cultivation, Seo Eun-hyun regresses life after life and keeps all of his memories every time. Although there are many catches and condition in there but in general, he regresses every time he dies and retains all of his memories. At one time, there’s so much memory piled up that it stops working like normal memory. At that point he is not strong enough to digest. So, It turns into instinct and feeling. He can’t narrate it or think it through anymore and just moves on instinct.

At the other end is Dostoevsky’s White Nights. The narrator is only ever called the dreamer. He’s lived alone in Petersburg for years and barely knows anyone. He talks to the houses he passes, and they answer him. When he meets a girl named Nastenka and she asks about him, he tells her he has no history. He even calls himself “a type”. Göncüoğlu reads him as a “superfluous man,” someone stuck in his own monologue with nobody to answer back.

These were the two extremes. Seo Eun-hyun has way too much memory. It basically turns him into pure instinct. But he’s still him. There’s still somebody there, moving through all of it. The dreamer’s the opposite. He says he has no history. Nothing to remember, nobody who knows him. But he isn’t blank either. Being alone is his identity. He talks to the houses, talks to the air, and then talks to Nastenka, and you can hear exactly who he is when he does. His personality comes out in the way he talks to her.

So one has way too much memory and the other says he has none, and both still have a personal identity. If memory is what makes you YOU, that shouldn’t happen. Something’s still there when the memory part is overloaded or basically missing. So what is that?

And that takes me here. The philosophers gave me three main answers. A soul, which is the old one. Memory, which is Locke. Psychological continuity, which is Parfit. And every one of them breaks in the same spot, when somebody gets copied, or stretched, or pushed to an extreme.

I don’t think that means any of them are just wrong. I think “personal identity” is being asked to do too many jobs at once. Locke needs it to decide who gets blamed and who doesn’t, cause it wouldn’t be fair to punish somebody for something a different person did. Parfit needs it to figure out what’s worth caring about in the future, like whether I should be scared of the teleporter or not. Roman’s friends needed it to decide whether a chatbot version of him counted as him at all. One answer was never and will never cover all of that.

And the two novels showed me something else. Even with way too much memory or basically none, there’s still somebody there. So memory can’t be the whole answer. I don’t think a soul is either, cause I still can’t say what it would even add. What I’m left with is personal identity itself, whatever that is. I can’t define it. But I can tell it’s there when I look at those two characters.

It also brings me back to where this started. I wanted to know if a soul survives death, but that question sits on top of this one. If a soul carried all my memories somewhere, Locke says that’s me. If it carried none of them, he says it isn’t, even if it’s the same soul. So whether “I” survive death depends on what “I” even means. The two novels make me think what’s left over isn’t memory, and I don’t think it’s a soul either, but it’s something.

So back to the start. Am I the same person I was a year ago? An hour ago? Around my mom versus my friends? I still don’t have a clean answer. What I have now is a better idea of why nobody does. Whatever technology shows up that can scan us or rebuild us, I don’t think it closes the question. It just moves the same question onto new stuff. I guess I’ll keep asking it.
I want to end with a poem I wrote that addresses what I think Personal Identity is:

A figure as white as meCircles slowly, as if watching us.Is it protecting us? Keeping watch over us?I ponder...

Topic Explanation

About this piece

This is my first real explanation of a topic, back when I was still on death and the soul. It covers what people believe, how experts approach it, and the works I'd read, like Plato's Phaedo and Kagan's Yale course. I chose it because death has been close to me and my family. I used to be scared of it, and reading regression novels and getting into philosophy helped.

The piece, as I wrote it

An Overview to Death and Soul

Death, something almost everyone is afraid of and wants to escape from it. But do people know what death exactly means? What does dying mean exactly aside from the biological aspect of the body shutting down? Do we have a soul? What happens after we die? There are many questions like this, and the answer will differ from person to person when asked. And through this topic I hope to put forth my perspective about death and also present what other philosophers such as Plato, Aristotle, Descartes think about death. I hope to unveil some of the secrets about death.

Public & Expert Approaches to Death and Soul

I think the public approach to death is not that proactive because most people already have a definition of their own and they don’t want to change that or even listen to others’ opinions. Even when they listen sometimes, they don’t take anything from it. This is especially true in a religious context, since different religions have very different views on death. For example, Christianity or Islam is not very open to the concept of death. On the other hand, Hinduism and Buddhism have a very open concept of death or nirvana.

However, experts are very open to this topic. They approach death through logic, fallacies and proofs. In this day of modern science where technology is constantly evolving, a breakthrough in some part about death or something could be discovered. That is why every expert is in search of evidence to make their own theory.

The Importance of Death and Soul

This topic could be important to religious people who believe there is a soul and that, after death, people go to heaven or hell. Or this could be important to people who are atheist or agnostic or anything similar and think that there is nothing after death, i.e., the soul doesn’t exist. This could be important to people who are searching for the meaning of death. What does it mean to die? For some people, it could just be curiosity or interest in this topic. As death itself is a very mysterious and closed topic to people, the reason for importance could vary according to the person’s interest.

Works Related to Death and Soul

The works related to death are numerous, and among them Phaedo by Plato is one of the first and a very good work related to death. The Phaedo is a dialogue in which Socrates argues for what the soul is and what happens after death. He tries to prove that the soul is immortal and I would say he was successful to some extent too. But he doesn’t explain where the soul comes from or prove the existence of the soul. His argument is only valid when a soul exists at all. But we don’t know if it exists.

And another discussion by Shelly Kagan in his Death course is just the opposite of phaedo. He talks about the physicalist view that the soul doesn’t exist. Everything is the working of our brain. The human body is just a body that can do a certain function and after our death, there is nothing. He takes out the loopholes in phaedo and analyses and discusses them and says how souls can’t exist.


Similarly, there are other philosophers like Descartes who wrote about how the soul and body are different but interact with each other. This is a similar but different concept to that of Plato’s.

My Reason for Selecting Death and Soul

Death has been very close to me and my family. Losing my father and cousin at a young age, getting seriously sick more than once, nearly dying from appendicitis, and losing my grandmother are times when death has been very close. And I used to be really afraid of death. Why did it happen to me? What did I do wrong? So many questions always used to linger in my mind and I always used to think about them. And that made me read novels related to regression, which showed me that even infinite lives don't matter as something always goes wrong even if you try to fix something. Anyways, reading novels, watching anime and reading manga helped me grasp some form of knowledge about death and I learned to not be scared of it. This led me to the curiosity toward philosophy and as I read and watched videos about the gods, universe and how things came to be and that also made me curious about death. And It hasn’t been that long since I have been exploring Death and through this class, I thought this would be the perfect opportunity to satisfy my curiosity deeply and also do the writing.

Annotated Bibliography

About this piece

This is where the topic changed. At first I was writing about death and the soul, but I'd only read Phaedo, some web novels, and a few short stories, and I couldn't connect them. While I was summarizing the web novel A Regressor's Tale of Cultivation for a citation, I realized its main character, who ends up with so much memory that he barely knows who he is, was asking something bigger: what makes you the same person? That tied the soul in Phaedo, White Nights, and the novel together. So I collected ten sources on personal identity, with what each one is, what interested me, and how I'd use it.

The piece, as I wrote it

Research Question

What is personal Identity? What defines you? Does it depend on the memory of a person, as Locke argues? But a person’s memory could be copied. Or does it reside in the soul, as Plato says in Phaedo? This essay/paper will discuss what personal identity is, and we will discuss it through papers related to AI, souls, physical body, web novels and even short stories. I will try to go in depth as I can, and I am having really fun exploring this topic, and I feel this resonates with me a lot. I am even thinking of talking about personal identity from the perspective of Love, which I hope will be fruitful (It is already fun).

Annotated Bibliography

Plato. Phaedo. Translated by Benjamin Jowett, Project Gutenberg, 29 Oct. 2008, https://www.gutenberg.org/files/1658/1658-h/1658-h.htm.

  • Description: This dialogue depicts Socrates's final hours before his execution, in which he offers several arguments for the soul's immortality, where he debates with his friends and gives his final teaching to his followers as he is freed from living.
  • Most interesting: The dialogue shifts between every person and Socrates, where he wins every argument and proves to them the soul is immortal. But he also talks about the afterlife, hell and rewards, which goes into mythology, and he says he cannot prove the exact, but he is sure the afterlife exists, and there is some sort of system in place.
  • Usefulness: The afterlife myth requires that a specific individual soul's memories and moral record persist for judgment to make sense. This argument is one of the weakest in the text, and this gap first pushed me to ask whether memory, rather than soul, is what actually secures personal identity.

  • Usefulness: This is my one and only case of personal identity under memory overload rather than memory loss, instead of too little memory or the memory transfer problem.

  • Description: A lonely, unnamed narrator known only as "the dreamer" meets a young woman, Nastenka, over four nights in St. Petersburg, falls in love with her, and is left alone again when her actual lover returns.
  • Most interesting: When Nastenka asks the narrator to tell her his history, he responds that he has none and that he has lived entirely alone, with no story to tell, directly raising the question of whether a person without an accessible personal history is fully a person at all.
  • Usefulness: This is my case of identity built on almost no memory rather than too much, giving me, alongside RTOC, both extremes of the memory spectrum to test Locke's theory against.

Göncüoğlu, M. Önder. "Lonely Dreamers in Dostoevsky's White Nights and Sabahattin Ali's Madonna in a Fur Coat: A Comparative Analysis." Ankara Üniversitesi Dil ve Tarih-Coğrafya Fakültesi Dergisi, vol. 57, no. 1, 2017, pp. 202–225.

  • Description: A comparative literary analysis situating the narrators of White Nights and Madonna in a Fur Coat within the Russian literary archetype of the "superfluous man," drawing on Bakhtin's concept of dialogism to argue their defining trait is imprisonment in monologue rather than genuine connection with others.

  • Description: Locke separates "person" from "man" and argues personal identity consists not in sameness of soul or body but in continuity of consciousness, chiefly memory, illustrated through his prince-and-cobbler thought experiment.
  • Most interesting: Locke calls "person" a forensic term, meant specifically to justly assign praise, blame, and punishment, tying personal identity directly to moral responsibility rather than metaphysics alone.
  • Usefulness: This is the theory my entire paper tests. If identity really is just continuity of memory, that content seems, at least in principle, copyable, which is exactly the possibility AI reconstructions put pressure on.

Parfit, Derek. Reasons and Persons. Oxford University Press, 1984, https://philarchive.org/archive/SAUITP.

  • Description: Using thought experiments involving teleportation and brain fission, Parfit argues that strict personal identity is not what matters in survival, only psychological continuity, which he calls Relation R, matters, and that this can hold in a one-to-many way with no determinate answer as to "which one is really me."
  • Most interesting: In his branch-line case, the original person does not immediately die after teleporting, so two equally valid people briefly coexist, one of whom is about to die, showing identity can break down without anything bad actually happening.
  • Usefulness: Supplies the sophisticated middle position between Plato's soul and Locke's simple memory theory, and gives me the specific concept, wholesale psychological continuity, that I test against both fictional and real AI reconstructions.

Newton, Casey. "Speak, Memory." The Verge, 6 Oct. 2016, www.theverge.com/a/luka-artificial-intelligence-memorial-roman-mazurenko-bot.

  • Description: Long-form journalism telling the true story of Eugenia Kuyda, who built a chatbot trained on thousands of text messages from her friend Roman Mazurenko after his sudden death, a project that later became the basis for the Replika app.
  • Most interesting: The mixed reactions from Mazurenko's own family and friends, where some were comforted and some unsettled, showing the philosophical question of whether the bot is “really him” playing out as a real, unresolved disagreement among people who actually knew him.
  • Usefulness: This is my concrete, documented case rather than a hypothetical, letting me analyze a real AI reconstruction using Locke’s, Parfit’s, and Roache’s frameworks instead of a purely invented scenario.

"Be Right Back." Black Mirror, season 2, episode 1, written by Charlie Brooker, directed by Owen Harris, Channel 4, 11 Feb. 2013.

  • Description: A grieving woman uses a service that reconstructs her deceased boyfriend from his digital footprint, first as a chatbot, then a voice, then a physical android, only to find the reconstruction unsettlingly compliant and without any real independent will.
  • Most interesting: The horror of the episode is not that the copy is unconvincing, it is that it is convincing enough to be disturbing precisely because it lacks the resistance, moods, and self-preservation instinct a real person would have.
  • Usefulness: Dramatizes, in concrete and emotional terms, the exact philosophical problem Roache identifies abstractly: a reconstruction built from fragments of a person's outputs rather than their whole continuous mind.

Roache, Rebecca. "A Defence of Quasi-Memory." Philosophy, vol. 81, no. 2, 2006, pp. 323–355.

  • Description: A philosophy journal article investigating whether "quasi-memory," a memory-like state not caused by one's own past experience, is a coherent concept, testing it against Locke's circularity problem and Parfit's use of it to argue that identity is not what matters in survival.
  • Most interesting: Roache distinguishes piecemeal memory-trace copying, which produces a weaker, impoverished q-memory lacking its original psychological context, from wholesale psychological continuity, arguing only the latter actually rescues the concept from objection.
  • Usefulness: Gives me the precise technical vocabulary and argument structure for my central claim: that AI reconstructions are structurally a piecemeal case, extracted information about a person rather than a full transfer of their mind, and therefore cannot do the identity-preserving work Locke's theory would need them to do.

Outline

About this piece

This is the plan for the essay: a thesis, then six steps with my sources under each one. It goes from Locke, to ordinary change over time, to duplication, to a real AI case, to the two extreme characters, and ends with what it all means. The follow-up covers what I was unsure about. The essay keeps the plan's main argument, that every theory breaks in the same place, and adds the Ship of Theseus and the social-self idea I'd left out. It drops the brain point, and it ends with something new: even with too much or too little memory, someone is still there.

The piece, as I wrote it

Thesis

So here is the question I cannot actually answer about myself: am I the same person I was a year ago? Yesterday? An hour ago? Am I even the same person in front of my mom as I am in front of my friends, on the same afternoon? I do not think any of the theories this essay looks at actually solve that for me, not the idea that some soul holds me together underneath it all, not Locke's claim that memory alone does the job, not even Parfit's claim that psychological continuity is really what matters. Every one of them falls apart the moment you push on it hard enough, whether that push is something as violent as an AI chatbot built out of a dead man's old texts, or something as ordinary as just living another day. I want to test these theories against my own uncertainty about which version of me is actually "me," against a real case of AI reconstruction, and against two literary extremes, a character with far too much memory to even hold onto, and a character with almost none at all. And I think the fact that every theory fails in the same place is more interesting than any one of them being simply wrong. Maybe the real problem is not that we are missing the right answer. Maybe it is that we are asking "personal identity" to do too many different jobs at once, justify blame, let us survive, let us be recognized, let us recognize ourselves, and no single theory was ever built to do all of that at the same time.

Outline

I. Starting with Locke, since he's the one who actually splits "person" away from both "man" and "soul" in the first place.

a. Walking through what Locke means by "man" (just the biological animal) versus "person" (something defined by consciousness), and why he calls personal identity a forensic term, basically a legal and moral label, not a metaphysical one.

i. Source: Locke, "Of Identity and Diversity"

b. Then Getting into the prince-and-cobbler thing to point out that Locke straight up says the same soul without shared memory would not make you the same person.

i. Source: Locke, "Of Identity and Diversity"

c. Now the obvious problem: if you define identity through memory, are not you just assuming the very identity you are trying to prove?

II. Now to test Locke's theory the slow way, through ordinary change over time, no copying or duplication needed at all.

a. As Heraclitus said, since "you cannot step in the same river twice" is basically this whole argument in one line, and it is 2,500 years old.

i. Source: Heraclitus, fragments on flux

b. And then Parfit's connectedness versus continuity distinction, the rope made of overlapping fibers, since that is the move that answers my own question.

i. Source: Parfit, Reasons and Persons

c. Again, Now mention that the brain stays more physically stable than the rest of the body, which is basically why people like Kagan think the brain is doing the real identity-carrying work.

d. Finally, Trying out my own idea: let’s treat a personality like an integral, adding up infinitely many instants into one whole, finite self. What will happen?

III. Now testing it the hard way, duplication, which is way more violent than just existing day to day.

a. Get into Parfit's fission and teleportation stuff, and his idea of Relation R, basically psychological continuity without needing strict identity.

i. Source: Parfit, Reasons and Persons

b. Then Bring in Roache's quasi-memory concept, why copying memories piecemeal gives you a weaker, emptier copy that is missing all the original context.

i. Source: Roache, "A Defence of Quasi-Memory"

c. Discussing the fact that the only version that actually works is a full, wholesale transfer, and that needs an empty mind to receive it, which might not even be a coherent thing to ask for in the first place.

i. Source: Roache, "A Defence of Quasi-Memory"

IV. Bringing in a real case instead of just hypotheticals, AI reconstructing someone after they die.

a. Eugenia Kuyda's chatbot, built from her dead friend Roman Mazurenko's old texts. This actually happened, it is not a thought experiment.

i. Source: Newton, "Speak, Memory"

b. Bring in Black Mirror's "Be Right Back" for the emotional, gut-punch version of the exact same thing.

i. Source: "Be Right Back"

c. Arguing about both of these as they are exactly the case Roache already showed does not hold up.

V. Testing the amount-of-memory question through two characters at total opposite extremes.

a. Seo Eun-hyun from A Regressor's Tale of Cultivation, who has so much memory from thousands of lifetimes that it stops being memory and just turns into instinct.

i. Source: A Regressor's Tale of Cultivation

b. The narrator of White Nights, who has basically no personal history at all, and flat out tells Nastenka he has none.

i. Source: Dostoevsky, White Nights

ii. Source: Göncüoğlu, "Lonely Dreamers in Dostoevsky's White Nights and Sabahattin Ali's Madonna in a Fur Coat"

c. Discussing how both extremes still have identity, or the lack of it, happening regardless of how much memory is actually there. So maybe the amount was never the real variable to begin with.

VI. Wrap it up: what does it actually mean that every single theory breaks at the same spot?

a. Soul, memory, psychological continuity, all of them fail right where duplication or extraction happens, even though they are built on completely different foundations.

b. Argue the real disagreement is not about evidence, it is about what we actually want identity to do, justify blame, let us survive, let us be recognized, let us recognize ourselves. Maybe no theory was ever meant to do all of that at once.

c. Come back to my own question from the start, and leave it open: could some future tech, like a full connectome scan, actually fix this, or would it just move the same unanswered question onto a new kind of material?

Follow Up

I’ve got this other idea floating around too, like, since you’re not the same you every single day, doesn’t that mean you’re not the same you around your family versus your friends either? So maybe social identity and personal identity are kind of evolving together, same process, not two separate things. But I haven’t found a solid place to actually put that yet, so I’m leaving it out for now. That’s basically it. I don’t really know what else to say, I just hope writing it ends up being as fun as planning it was.
